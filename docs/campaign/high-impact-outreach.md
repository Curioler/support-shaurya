# High-impact outreach — scaffold

**Status:** structure and leads only. **The research pass has not been done.** Nothing below
is verified; every scheme, amount and contact needs checking before use.

**Purpose:** identify and approach the small number of parties who could move the needle by
a lot, rather than adding more people who might give ₹1,000.

---

## Start from the arithmetic

₹26 crore has been raised by public crowdfunding in India before, but rarely, and almost
never from small donations alone. At the current rate — ₹11.5 lakh in about three weeks
across two platforms — the campaign reaches roughly ₹2 crore in a year.

That is not an argument to stop crowdfunding. It is an argument about **what this outreach
is for.** It should be aimed at three things, in this order:

1. **A small number of very large gifts.** One corporate CSR commitment or one benefactor
   changes the picture more than a hundred thousand shares.
2. **Statutory and institutional funds** the family may simply not know exist.
3. **Amplification** — reach that converts into the above, not reach for its own sake.

Chasing volume for its own sake is how campaigns exhaust their network and stall.

## Category A — Government schemes *(research first, highest priority)*

The strongest lead, and one nobody in the documents has mentioned:

> **India's National Policy for Rare Diseases (2021)** provides financial assistance for
> treatment of listed rare diseases, disbursed through designated **Centres of Excellence**,
> with a per-patient ceiling in the region of **₹50 lakh**. **Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy is
> a listed disorder.** There is also a government-hosted crowdfunding portal for rare
> disease patients.
>
> ⚠ Amounts, eligibility and the current portal all need verifying — the policy has been
> amended more than once.

**Why this matters beyond the money:** applying through a Centre of Excellence puts Shaurya
into a formal national register of rare-disease patients. That is how families hear about
managed-access programmes, and how they get counted.

- [ ] Confirm the current scheme, ceiling and application process
- [ ] Identify the nearest **Centre of Excellence** — likely candidates in Bengaluru
      (NIMHANS) or Ahmedabad; needs checking
- [ ] Check whether Dr Ann Agnes Mathew or SPARSH can refer directly
- [ ] **Rashtriya Arogya Nidhi** and the **Health Minister's Cancer Patient Fund** style
      schemes — check what applies to non-cancer rare disease
- [ ] **Gujarat Chief Minister's Relief Fund** — the family is in Vadodara; state relief
      funds handle medical grants and are usually more reachable than central ones
- [ ] **Karnataka CM Relief Fund** — treatment is at SPARSH, Bengaluru; check whether
      treatment-state eligibility applies
- [ ] **PMNRF** (Prime Minister's National Relief Fund) — medical assistance grants exist but
      are typically modest against this figure. Worth applying, not worth waiting on
- [ ] **PM-CARES** — check whether it has any individual medical assistance route at all

*A realistic note: these schemes together are unlikely to exceed ₹1 crore. They matter
because they are real money the family is probably entitled to, and because a government
grant is powerful social proof for every other approach.*

      `SareptAlly@sarepta.com`, 1-888-SAREPTA

- [ ] Ask whether **outcome-based or instalment arrangements** are available in any market

*Manufacturers of ultra-expensive therapies frequently have access programmes that are not
advertised. The worst outcome is a no, and it costs one email. This should arguably sit in
the blocking list, not here.*

## Category C — Corporate CSR *(most realistic route to a very large single gift)*

Indian companies above a size threshold must spend **2% of average net profit on CSR**, and
healthcare qualifies. Many are actively looking for credible beneficiaries near year-end.

- [ ] Build a list of **Gujarat-headquartered** corporates and their CSR contacts —
      the family's home state is the strongest hook
- [ ] Add **Vadodara industrial belt** employers specifically
- [ ] Add pharma and healthcare companies, for whom a rare-disease child is on-mission
- [ ] Check whether Gaurang's own employer has a CSR or employee-hardship fund
- [ ] Prepare a **one-page CSR proposal** — different document from a crowdfunding appeal:
      audited need, hospital certification, fund-utilisation mechanism, 80G/tax position

*CSR is the category most likely to produce a single seven-figure cheque, and the one where
the family's current materials are least suited. It needs its own document.*

*These rarely give money. They give something more useful: navigation. They have done the
government paperwork before, they know which CoE responds, and they know which families got
funded and how.*

writes a shorter, more cautious piece — or none.*

## Category F — Where not to spend effort

Recorded so the effort is not wasted twice:

- **WHO** does not fund individual patients. Nor does UNICEF. These are normative and
  programmatic bodies, not grant-makers to families.
- **Foreign embassies** and international aid bodies do not fund individual foreign nationals'
  medical treatment.
- **Mass-tagging celebrities on social media** has a very low yield and, done at volume from
  the family's accounts, carries the same platform risk that just cost Gaurang his WhatsApp.

## Before any of this goes out — prerequisites

High-impact approaches get one attempt. A CSR head or a Chief Minister's office reads the
material once. So these must be true first:

- [ ] **One goal figure** across all channels *(currently ₹20 crore vs ₹26 crore)*
- [ ] **Combined running total** published somewhere citable
- [ ] Campaign page **errors corrected**
- [ ] A **one-page summary** with the diagnosis, the certificate, the cost and the mechanism
      for funds — institutional donors will not read a crowdfunding page
- [ ] **Named lead clinician** and a single point of contact for the campaign
- [ ] A **working phone number** — the banned WhatsApp number may be on printed material

*Doing the outreach before these are fixed spends the family's best opportunities on their
weakest presentation.*
